Generalizability (G-Study) and Decision (D-Study) Planning

1. Crossed Observed-Score Generalizability and Decision Studies

Suppose an assessment team can afford eight ratings per person. Would two raters using four criteria or four raters using two criteria give more precise scores? A G-Study estimates how observed-score variation is distributed across persons, raters, criteria and their interactions. A D-Study uses those estimated components to project precision under candidate rating plans; no latent-trait MFRM fit is required.

First decide what the score is for. Use \(G\) for relative decisions, such as ranking persons, and \(\Phi\) for absolute decisions against a fixed standard. Systematic rater or criterion differences enter absolute error even when they do not change relative ordering. Neither coefficient is a probability that an individual pass/fail decision is correct.

The bundled example_core contains 1-to-4 ratings. For this illustration we treat both raters and criteria as random samples from the intended assessment universe. This assumption matters: deleting fixed, substantively required rubric criteria changes what is assessed and is not justified by this D-Study.

library(mfrmr)

ratings <- load_mfrmr_data("example_core")

gt <- mfrm_g_study(
  ratings,
  scores = "Score",
  person = "Person",
  facets = c("Rater", "Criterion"),
  method = "urgenova",
  d_study_grid = expand.grid(Rater = c(1, 2, 3, 4), Criterion = c(2, 3, 4))
)
print(gt)

Read the equal-budget rows together: the larger relevant coefficient and the smaller corresponding SEM indicate greater projected precision. AbsSEM is on the original score scale, whereas \(G\) and \(\Phi\) are variance ratios. These rows answer the allocation question only under the same universe and scoring conditions as the G-Study. Costs, content coverage and feasible assignments can still rule out the statistically more precise plan.

For these data, four raters with two criteria give \(\Phi = 0.777\), compared with 0.768 for two raters with four criteria. Thus the former has a slightly higher point projection for absolute decisions. Both are below 0.80, an illustrative planning target rather than a universal acceptability threshold. The difference is too small to establish a preferred design from these point estimates alone.

Inspect gt$variance_components and confint(gt) before relying on small differences. Negative raw component estimates remain visible in RawEstimate and are truncated to zero in the default projections. The reported intervals use a Gaussian plug-in covariance and Satterthwaite or delta approximations; they do not provide exact small-sample coverage. In this example, Person:Criterion has an estimate much smaller than its SE. Its Satterthwaite degrees of freedom are below 1, so the approximate interval is withheld with status unstable_satterthwaite_df. This reporting guard avoids presenting an extremely large, unstable upper bound as informative uncertainty. It does not establish coverage for the remaining components. An unavailable interval is NA, not zero uncertainty. mfrm_d_study() reports point projections, so this table alone does not establish that one plan is reliably superior. See ?mfrm_g_study for assumptions and ?mfrm_d_study for the output definitions.

2. Nested Designs and Multivariate Composite Weights

The next example answers a different question: how dependable is a prespecified 60% Content / 40% Organization composite when each task has its own rater panel? Declaring Rater nested within Task represents that assignment; treating the panels as crossed would describe a different design. The component covariances matter because the two subscores come from the same ratings. Specify the weights from the assessment purpose before inspecting reliability:

nested_ratings <- load_mfrmr_data("example_gtheory_nested")
head(nested_ratings, 4)
#>   Person Task Rater Organization    Content
#> 1      1    1    R1    -1.794591 -2.7166217
#> 2      2    1    R1     1.728080  1.5039467
#> 3      3    1    R1     1.224649  1.2985221
#> 4      4    1    R1     1.166801 -0.1208148

gt_nested <- mfrm_g_study(
  nested_ratings,
  scores = c("Content", "Organization"),
  weights = c(0.6, 0.4),
  person = "Person",
  facets = c("Rater", "Task"),
  nesting = c(Rater = "Task"),
  method = "urgenova"
)
print(gt_nested)
#> Generalizability (G-Study) / Decision (D-Study) [Henderson Method 1 (urGENOVA) | N = 540 ratings, 30 persons | 0.1230 s]

Explicit composite weights retain their scale: c(1, 1) defines a sum and c(.5, .5) an average. Multiplying all weights by two doubles SEM and quadruples variance components without changing G or Phi. Omitted weights define an equal-weight average.

The composite summary answers the question for the stated weights, while gt_nested$univariate describes the separate subscores. A dependable composite does not establish that either subscore supports individual reporting. When planning further ratings with mfrm_d_study(gt_nested, design_grid = ...), a Rater count is per task, not the total number across tasks. The nested design, score definitions and intended populations must remain appropriate for the proposed assessment.
